Lake Lanier facts: what sailors should know before they sail

Captain John Rice · 2026-10-02

By Captain John Rice, Captain & Owner, Lord Nelson Charters · 2026-09-30 · 10 min read

A guest at Dock Zk asked me last April how deep the water sat under Browns Bridge. Fair question, and it is the kind of thing that changes what a sail looks like. The Lake Lanier facts that matter most to sailors are few: the lake filled behind Buford Dam starting in 1956, it covers about 38,000 acres at full pool, and the Army Corps decides how high it sits.

Key takeaways

  • Buford Dam closed the Chattahoochee River and Lake Lanier began filling in 1956.
  • Surface area at full pool runs about 38,000 acres and the shoreline measures 692 miles, per the U.S. Army Corps of Engineers.
  • Water level swings around a full pool mark of 1,071 feet above sea level, a figure set and managed by the U.S. Army Corps of Engineers.
  • Lake level readings that sailors use to check clearance under spans like Browns Bridge are published by the Lake Lanier Association.
  • The Lake Lanier facts that change an actual sail day are level, wind, and season, and the wind piece comes from the National Weather Service.

Lake Lanier facts start in 1956, when Buford Dam closed

Lake Lanier is man-made and not very old. The U.S. Army Corps of Engineers completed Buford Dam on the Chattahoochee River in 1956, and water backed up behind it over the following years to form the reservoir we sail today. Flood control, water supply, and hydropower were the reasons; recreation came along with them.

The dam sits at the south end of the lake and is still operated by the U.S. Army Corps of Engineers civil works program that runs federal reservoirs like Lake Sidney Lanier. That concrete is the only reason there is water here to sail on. Before it, this was the Chattahoochee running through farmland and small crossroads communities that were bought out as the water came up. Most Lake Lanier facts you hear at a marina bar start right there, with a dam and a date.

Guests ask about the towns under the water on almost every charter. Some of that is documented and some has grown in the retelling, but the plain version is that the valley was cleared and flooded on purpose in the middle 1950s. Two of the Lake Lanier facts I repeat most often on the boat are about age and about purpose. This is a working reservoir first. Flood control on the Chattahoochee and water supply for metro Atlanta sit above anyone's Saturday sail in the order of operations. Sailing happens inside those rules, not around them.

Lake Lanier facts about size: how big is Lake Lanier, and what it means for a charter

At full pool the lake covers roughly 38,000 acres and holds 692 miles of shoreline, according to U.S. Army Corps of Engineers figures. Those two numbers explain most of what a charter day feels like. The water is wide enough for real wind and cut up enough to give you a quiet cove almost anywhere.

For scale, that shoreline beats Georgia's entire general Atlantic coastline of about 100 miles, per NOAA shoreline mileage tables, by close to seven times.

A 692 mile shoreline wrapped around 38,000 acres means the edge is very long relative to the surface. In plain terms, the lake is all fingers, points, and creek mouths, and Two Mile Creek alone runs back nearly a mile from the main channel, one of dozens of arms shaped like it. That shape is why the breeze shifts and lifts the way it does here, and why two boats a mile apart can be sailing different wind. The Lake Lanier facts about size are not trivia; they set how far we get and what we see. It is also why a half day sail and a full day sail cover different water instead of the same loop twice, which I broke down in the guide to half-day versus full-day charter length.

Size spreads people out, too. Lanier is a busy lake, and on a July Saturday the ramps and the main channel show it. Past the no-wake zones, though, tens of thousands of acres thin that traffic quickly. These are the Lake Lanier facts that surprise first-time guests most: you can leave a full marina and be alone with the sails up inside twenty minutes.

Lake Lanier facts about water level and who controls it

The Corps of Engineers sets the water level, not the weather alone. Full pool on Lake Lanier is 1,071 feet above sea level, and the Corps raises and lowers the lake around that mark for flood storage, downstream flow, and drought management. Sailors feel those few feet more than powerboaters do.

Full pool is a target, not a promise. In a wet spring the Corps pulls the lake down to make room for runoff, and in a dry fall it holds water back. The practical tool for a sailor is a current reading, and the Lake Lanier Association, the lake's nonprofit steward that publishes lake level updates, is where I look before a sail. A few feet of change moves the waterline against fixed structures, so clearance under a span like Browns Bridge is a number to check rather than assume.

Level is one half. Air is the other, and the National Weather Service forecast office in Peachtree City that covers north Georgia is the first thing I open in the morning. Thunderstorm timing in July and the gradient wind behind a March front change a charter plan more than any Lake Lanier facts about acreage do. Of the Lake Lanier facts on this page, water level is the one that moves week to week, so it is worth checking the day before you sail. Landmarks and Lake Lanier facts you can see from the deck

Most of my charters work the southern end of the lake. We leave Aqualand Marina from Dock Zk, clear the no-wake zone off Flowery Branch, and pick a line toward Two Mile Creek or up toward Browns Bridge depending on the wind. Buford Dam sits south of us, out of sight but always in the story.

Flowery Branch is the closest town to the slip, and the City of Flowery Branch, which manages the parks and public access on that side of the lake, is a useful stop for anyone building a day around a sail. Browns Bridge carries Georgia 369 across the middle of the lake and gives the clearest sense of scale you get from deck level: open water both directions and shoreline that keeps going. The Lake Lanier facts guests remember afterward are usually these visual ones rather than the acreage. I mapped the regular route in the post on what you see from Captain John's boat.

Lake Lanier facts about access: why the lake's size makes private sailing work

A lake this big with this much shoreline is what lets a private charter stay private. There is room for the party boats and the fishing tournaments and still a cove where fourteen people can hear each other talk. Small lakes cannot do both at once. Lanier can, most days.

The other half is access. The lake sits about an hour up I-985 from Atlanta, and Explore Georgia, the state tourism office, lists it among the state's main freshwater destinations. That drive is short enough that guests arrive rested instead of frayed, and I laid out the timing in planning a sailing day trip up I-985.

Wind is the last piece. A wide lake with 692 miles of ragged edge makes breeze that is workable but never perfectly steady, which suits guests fine: enough heel to feel like sailing, rarely enough to feel like work. Even inside a single afternoon, the wind can swing thirty or forty degrees off a wooded point without any front moving through, which is part of what keeps a sail here active instead of one long straight run. Georgia's rules still apply out there, and I keep a plain summary of them in the guide to what Georgia law requires before you sail. Of everything I hand people before a booking, one fact lands hardest: the lake is big enough to leave the crowd behind and small enough that we are back at the dock when we said we would be. That combination is what the Lake Lanier facts above add up to.

Frequently asked questions

How was Lake Lanier created, and when?

Lake Lanier is a man-made reservoir, not a natural lake. The U.S. Army Corps of Engineers built Buford Dam across the Chattahoochee River northeast of Atlanta, completed it in 1956, and the water rose behind it to flood the river valley over the following few years. Farms, roads, and small communities were bought out and cleared first. The dam still does the work it was built for: flood control, water supply for metro Atlanta, and hydropower. The Corps of Engineers Mobile District, which operates Buford Dam and the reservoir, runs it today.

How big is Lake Lanier?

At full pool the lake covers about 38,000 acres of surface water with 692 miles of shoreline, according to U.S. Army Corps of Engineers figures. For scale, that shoreline runs close to seven times the length of Georgia's general Atlantic coastline. It is the largest lake in the Atlanta region, and the Atlanta Convention and Visitors Bureau lists it among the outdoor destinations north of the city. From a sailboat, the number you feel is the shoreline rather than the acreage, because all those creek arms are what give you shelter and shifting wind.

Who decides how high the water sits on Lake Lanier?

The U.S. Army Corps of Engineers does. Full pool is 1,071 feet above sea level, and the Corps holds the lake near that mark or draws it down for flood storage, downstream water needs, and drought management. Rainfall sets what the Corps has to work with, and the National Weather Service, which publishes the rainfall and drought outlooks for Georgia, is the upstream part of that story. For sailors, the practical effect is clearance under fixed spans and depth in the back of coves. A few feet either way changes where a keelboat can go.

What do I need to bring for safety on a Lake Lanier charter?

Nothing special. The boat carries the required life jackets and safety gear, and I check it before we leave the slip. Georgia law sets the rules on children's life jackets, boating under the influence, and required equipment, and the Georgia Department of Natural Resources boating pages spell them out if you want to read ahead. The U.S. Coast Guard recreational boating safety office publishes the national equipment standards those state rules follow. What you should bring is sun protection, soft-soled shoes, water, and a light layer for the ride back after sunset.

What should I know about the lake before I book a sail?

Three things: the lake is big, the wind is shifty, and the water level moves. Size means a half day and a full day show you very different water. Shifty wind means a sail here stays active rather than running one straight line, which is part of why guests enjoy it. Water level means the Corps of Engineers, not the forecast alone, sets the shoreline you see that week. American Sailing, the national sailing education organization, publishes plain descriptions of how a keelboat sails if you want a picture of it first.

Can a complete beginner enjoy a sailing charter on Lake Lanier?

Yes. No experience is needed on a private charter. I sail the boat; you sit, take the wheel if you want to, and ask whatever you like. The lake helps, because inland water stays flatter than coastal water and the fetch across Lanier is short enough that waves rarely build into anything uncomfortable. US Sailing, the national governing body for the sport, keeps beginner resources if you want to read about points of sail first, but nobody needs to study before a charter. Comfortable shoes and sun protection matter more than knowledge.
