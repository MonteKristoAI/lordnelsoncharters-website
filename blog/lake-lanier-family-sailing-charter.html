Lake Lanier family sailing charter: a parent's planning guide

Captain John Rice · 2026-10-21

By Captain John Rice, Captain & Owner, Lord Nelson Charters · 2026-10-21 · 9 min read

Stand on Dock Zk at Aqualand Marina on a June morning and you can pick out the nervous parent right away: the one holding a four-year-old's hand and measuring the gap between dock and hull. Booking a Lake Lanier family sailing charter really comes down to four decisions, and life jackets are only the first one. Georgia DNR settles that question for anyone under 13. The rest is timing, shade, food, and a route that can shrink when the wind does.

Key takeaways

  • Children under age 13 must wear an appropriately sized PFD while a vessel is underway, unless they are inside a fully enclosed, roofed cabin, per Georgia DNR.
  • Sailboats over 12 feet used on Georgia waters have to be registered, so ask for the hull numbers before you pay a deposit, per Georgia DNR.
  • Anyone born after January 1, 1998 must complete an approved boating education course to operate a motorized vessel in Georgia, per Georgia DNR.
  • A Lake Lanier family sailing charter is calmer in the morning or the last hours before sunset, when heat and storm chances drop, per the National Weather Service forecast office in Peachtree City.
  • Lake Sidney Lanier is a federal reservoir whose levels and dam releases are managed day to day by the U.S. Army Corps of Engineers.

What should parents ask before booking a Lake Lanier family sailing charter?

Ask four questions before you book. Who is actually captaining the boat, is the sailboat registered with the state, are there life jackets on board in your children's sizes, and what happens to your date if a storm sits over Buford Dam. A good operator answers all four in one reply.

Registration is the easiest item to verify. Georgia DNR requires registration for sailboats over 12 feet used on Georgia waters, and the Georgia DNR boating regulations cover the numbering and decal rules that come with it. Numbers should be visible on the bow when you walk down the dock. A boat with no numbers and no explanation is a boat to skip.

Second, ask who is responsible for the boat. Georgia DNR states that people born after January 1, 1998, must complete an approved boating education course to operate a motorized vessel, a requirement laid out on the Georgia Department of Natural Resources boating education pages. For a Lake Lanier family sailing charter, the real question is simpler than the rulebook: who is steering, and how many seasons have they spent on this water? I have sailed out of Aqualand Marina for more than 20 years, and I take every charter out myself. There is no rotating roster of fill-in drivers.

Third, ask what is aboard in your children's sizes. Georgia DNR requires children under age 13 to wear an appropriately sized PFD while a vessel is underway, unless they are inside a fully enclosed, roofed cabin. Appropriately sized is the part parents forget to confirm, and a loose adult vest on a 40-pound child is worse than nothing. Our post on what Georgia law requires before you sail walks through the equipment list in more detail.

Fourth, ask about weather. The answer you want is not we sail rain or shine. You want a captain who will move your hour or your date and say so in writing. I moved a family's Saturday afternoon sail to Sunday morning two summers ago when a storm line stalled over Buford Dam, and told them by text the night before rather than making them guess at the dock. They thanked me for the warning, not for a sail that would have meant standing under a mast in a thunderstorm.

How to pick a comfortable time and route for a Lake Lanier family sailing charter

Pick the morning or the last three hours before sunset, and pick a route that can shrink. Midday in July bakes a cockpit and builds storms. Early and late give you softer light, cooler air, and water that weekend powerboat traffic has not chopped up yet.

Afternoon heating is the local pattern that ruins family plans. The National Weather Service forecast office in Peachtree City covers north Georgia, and in summer its forecasts routinely carry afternoon and evening thunderstorm chances that climb as the day warms. A 9:00 a.m. departure from Aqualand Marina usually beats that window. A 5:00 p.m. departure usually follows it.

Water level matters more here than on most lakes. Lake Sidney Lanier is operated by the U.S. Army Corps of Engineers Mobile District, which manages releases at Buford Dam for flood control, power, and downstream flow. Lower water shifts where shallow ground sits off island shores, which shifts where I am willing to anchor with kids aboard.

Route is the other lever. A Lake Lanier family sailing charter does not need to cross the whole lake. The water between Aqualand Marina and the Browns Bridge area gives plenty of open sailing, and Two Mile Creek offers a protected pocket when the main body kicks up or a child decides they have had enough breeze. Our rundown of islands, parks, and scenic shores shows the options, and the notes on when captains delay a charter explain how I make that call.

What children should wear and bring on a Lake Lanier family sailing charter

Closed-toe shoes with grip, a hat that ties on, sunscreen applied before you leave the car, and a life jacket that fits. Everything else is comfort. Cotton gets cold once it is wet, so quick-dry layers beat jeans, and a light long sleeve beats a tank top by the third hour.

Fit is the whole ballgame on life jackets. The U.S. Coast Guard Office of Boating Safety publishes guidance on choosing and fitting life jackets by weight and chest size, and the dock test takes ten seconds: fasten every buckle, then lift the child by the shoulder straps. If the jacket rides up toward their ears, it is too large. Sun on open water hits harder than sun on a soccer field, and National Weather Service heat safety guidance is worth reading before an August booking.

Spring and fall add one planning wrinkle: dress in one more layer than you think you need for a Lake Lanier family sailing charter, since air over the water runs cooler than the parking lot at Aqualand Marina. Our dress guide for charters covers the seasonal swing month by month.

How a captain keeps a Lake Lanier family sailing charter interesting for different ages

Give every child a job and the day changes. Toddlers get a shade spot and a cup. Six-year-olds steer with my hand next to theirs on the tiller. Ten-year-olds trim a sheet and learn which side the wind is coming from. Teenagers want the helm and a photo.

Short tasks repeated often beat one long explanation, which is how beginner sailing instruction is usually built. I keep the first twenty minutes busy: hoisting, coiling, calling out the red and green marks, watching for the wake of the Browns Bridge traffic. After that, attention wanders and that is fine. Kids go below, look for herons in the coves, or hang over the lifelines counting islands.

Lake context helps older children care. The Lake Lanier Association works on water quality and shoreline cleanups across the reservoir, and a ten-minute talk about where the water comes from, where it goes past Buford Dam, and what lives along the shoreline sticks better than a sail trim lecture. Most parents figure out by hour two that the best part of the day for many kids is not the sailing itself. It is anchoring in a quiet cove and swimming off the stern.

Snacks, restrooms, and motion comfort on a Lake Lanier family sailing charter

Food and bathrooms decide the mood more than wind speed does. Pack a soft cooler with more water than you think you need, keep glass off the boat, and plan a restroom stop at the marina right before boarding. Salty snacks travel better than sugary ones on a warm deck.

Hydration is the quiet one. Kids on open water sweat without noticing because the breeze dries them off, so I hand out water on a schedule rather than waiting for anyone to ask. Fruit, crackers, pretzels, and sandwiches all work. Melty chocolate and loose chips do not.

Motion is the worry parents raise most, and it is usually heel rather than nausea. A sailboat leaning over feels dramatic the first time, and a child who is told it is coming handles it far better than one surprised by it. I watch the forecast winds published by NOAA and its National Weather Service before deciding how much sail to carry with young kids aboard, and I will shorten sail to flatten the boat the moment someone looks uneasy. Seats near the middle of the boat, eyes on the shoreline, water, and a cracker handle nearly everything else. Our piece on whether Lake Lanier makes you seasick covers the details. A Lake Lanier family sailing charter in the morning gives you the flattest water of the day for a first try.

Frequently asked questions

Do my kids have to wear life jackets the whole time we are sailing?

If they are under 13, yes, any time the boat is underway. Georgia DNR requires children under age 13 to wear an appropriately sized PFD while a vessel is underway unless they are inside a fully enclosed, roofed cabin. US Sailing, the national governing body for the sport, pushes the same wear-it habit for adults in its safety education work. On my boat the whole family wears one for the first stretch out of Aqualand Marina so no child feels singled out. Adults can decide after that. Children keep theirs on until we are tied up again.

What is the best age to bring a child on a sailboat?

Three and up handles a short sail well with a parent within arm's reach, and six and up can take a real turn at the tiller. American Sailing builds its introductory instruction around short, repeated tasks, which is how young kids stay interested. Infants can sail, but shade, feeding, and life jacket fit all get harder, so think honestly about nap schedules. For a Lake Lanier family sailing charter with a toddler aboard, a two to three hour morning window beats a long afternoon on the water.

Will my kids get seasick on Lake Lanier?

Far less often than on the ocean. Lake Sidney Lanier is an inland reservoir operated by the U.S. Army Corps of Engineers, so there is no ocean swell, and chop here is short and local, mostly boat wakes on a busy Saturday. What bothers kids is usually heel, the boat leaning under sail, rather than motion sickness. I reduce sail and flatten the boat when a child looks uneasy. Dry crackers, water, eyes on the shoreline, and a seat near the middle of the boat settle most of it.

Is there a restroom, and can we bring our own food?

Bring your own food and drinks. I ask for no glass and a soft-sided cooler that stows under a seat. For restrooms, plan on the marina before you board, and ask me directly about the facilities on board when you book rather than assuming. Families often add a stop in Flowery Branch, the Hall County city next to Aqualand Marina, for lunch or a park break before or after a sail. With young children, a bathroom stop fifteen minutes before departure is the best scheduling move you can make.

What happens if a thunderstorm shows up?

We move. North Georgia storms build fast in summer, and National Weather Service lightning safety guidance is blunt: there is no safe place outside when lightning is in the area, and a sailboat with a tall aluminum mast is not an exception. I watch radar before every departure and will shorten the route, change the departure hour, or reschedule the date. Families get that decision as early as I can make it rather than at the dock. No sail is worth sitting under a mast while a cell crosses Buford Dam.

How far is Aqualand Marina from Atlanta, and when should we arrive?

Aqualand Marina sits in Flowery Branch, reached from Atlanta by heading north on I-985, and Atlanta Convention and Visitors Bureau trip planning places the Lake Lanier area within an easy day trip of the city. Give yourself buffer for I-85 and I-985 traffic, then plan to be at Dock Zk about twenty minutes before your start time. That buffer covers parking, a restroom stop, sunscreen, and life jacket fitting. Arriving on the hour for a Lake Lanier family sailing charter means spending your first fifteen minutes on logistics instead of sailing.
